# **Backpropagation and Biologically Plausible Gradient Descent Algorithms - Review Paper**

Backpropagation has been the engine behind virtually every major advance in deep learning over the past three decades. It works remarkably well — but there's a persistent and increasingly important question about whether it has anything to do with how the brain actually learns. The short answer, based on current neuroscience, is probably not.

Three specific problems have been well-documented. Backpropagation requires the exact transpose of the forward weight matrices during the backward pass — a constraint called the weight transport problem — yet biological synapses are predominantly unidirectional and show no such reciprocal symmetry. It also requires a strict separation between forward and backward computation phases, whereas real neurons operate continuously and in parallel. Perhaps most fundamentally, it relies on global error signals that must travel backward through every layer of the network, a mechanism with no clear biological counterpart given that synaptic plasticity in the brain is driven by local signals — most notably the spike-timing relationships described by STDP.

These aren't minor implementation details. They reflect a fundamental mismatch between how artificial networks are trained and how biological learning systems are thought to operate. This gap matters both scientifically — for understanding the brain — and practically, as the field increasingly looks toward neuromorphic hardware and energy-efficient on-device learning where backpropagation's global, phase-dependent computation becomes a real constraint.

This paper reviews the most significant attempts to close that gap. We cover Hebbian learning and spike-timing dependent plasticity as the foundational local learning rules; target propagation and feedback alignment as direct structural alternatives to backpropagation; predictive coding and dendritic computation as frameworks grounded in cortical neuroscience; and equilibrium propagation and the forward-forward algorithm as more recent approaches with strong theoretical underpinnings. For each, we analyze the core mechanism, the biological motivations, and the known limitations. We close with a comparative analysis across three plausibility criteria — weight asymmetry, local error computation, and single-phase training — and identify where the field's most important open problems lie.
